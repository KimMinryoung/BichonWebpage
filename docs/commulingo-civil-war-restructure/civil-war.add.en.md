## Building a Red Army with the time bought at Brest: from Sviyazhsk to Kazan

The peace of 3 March 1918 was a retreat chosen under German attack by a Soviet government whose army had dissolved. Lenin argued for immediate peace, Bukharin for revolutionary war and Trotsky for ending the war without signing; after the German offensive of February Lenin's line prevailed and the Left SRs left the government. The negotiations, the party debate and Germany's occupation of Ukraine, the Baltic and Finland are covered in [Brest-Litovsk](/commulingo/events/brest-litovsk). What matters here is what was built in the eight months that peace bought.

The founding decree of 28 January rested on the volunteer principle. By spring some 200,000 had enrolled, but most were unemployed men and returning soldiers drawn by rations and pay, and the only formations that deserved the name of fighting units were the Latvian Riflemen and a few workers' detachments. Trotsky, commissar for war from 13 March, turned this army inside out. Former general staff officers joined the Supreme Military Council created on 4 March; on 22 April the election of commanders was abolished and "universal military training" made eight weeks of drill compulsory for workers. On 29 May the All-Russian Central Executive Committee decreed conscription, and on 12 June the first call-up went out in 51 districts of the Volga and Urals. To the party's charge that he was "bringing back the tsar's generals" Trotsky answered with the political commissar. The order of 6 April defined the commissar as someone who "remains the embodiment of revolutionary duty even at the most critical moment", and no commander's order was valid without the commissar's countersignature. Some 75,000 former imperial officers served in the Red Army during the civil war, and most divisional and army commanders were among them.

The army's first test came on the Volga. The Czechoslovak Legion and the People's Army of Komuch took Samara in June and Simbirsk in July, and on 7 August Vladimir Kappel's troops seized Kazan in a surprise attack, along with the imperial gold reserve of 650 million roubles that had been moved there. Kazan was 800 kilometres by rail from Moscow, and between them there was in effect no Red army. On the evening of 7 August Trotsky left Moscow in the armoured train that would become his emblem and two days later set up headquarters at the small station of Sviyazhsk, west of Kazan. The 5th Army he found was a force in flight. When a White raid reached almost to Sviyazhsk at the end of August, a Petrograd workers' regiment fled downriver on a steamer without orders; Trotsky had 27 men, including the commander and the commissar, tried by military court and shot. The principle that if the soldiers ran the commissar would be shot first, and if the commissar ran the commander, was established here. At the same time he brought communists, cadets and Baltic sailors from Moscow to fill the ranks, and sent Raskolnikov's Volga flotilla against the White ships at Kazan.

The counterattack began in the week Lenin was shot in Moscow on 30 August. On 10 September the 5th Army and the Volga flotilla retook Kazan, and on 12 September Gaya Gai's Iron Division retook Simbirsk. Gai's telegram to Lenin, "the recapture of your native Simbirsk is the first answer to your wound", became the opening line of the Soviet war narrative. With the fall of Samara on 7 October Komuch's People's Army was pushed beyond the Urals, and the SRs' "third way" was left on the road that led from Ufa to Kolchak's Omsk. On 2 September the Central Executive Committee had proclaimed the republic an "armed camp" and created the Revolutionary Military Council, with Trotsky as chairman and Ioakim Vatsetis, commander of the Latvian Riflemen, as the first commander-in-chief. The Red Army grew to 800,000 at the end of 1918, three million at the end of 1919 and 5.5 million in 1920. Its actual fighting strength never exceeded a fifth of that and deserters numbered more than 1.7 million in 1919 alone, but the mere existence of a regular army with central mobilization, supply and command was an advantage the other side never had.

The argument inside the party over the nature of this army erupted at the Eighth Congress in March 1919. The "Military Opposition", including Smirnov and Voroshilov, attacked the use of former officers and the restoration of discipline as erasing the revolutionary character of the army; Lenin and Sokolnikov, speaking in Trotsky's absence, replied that no war had ever been won without military specialists. The congress endorsed the regular army while strengthening the commissar system and party control. This combination of professional knowledge and party supervision remained the skeleton of the Soviet army long after the civil war.

## The south: from the Ice March to Tsaritsyn, the Volunteer Army and the Don Cossacks in 1918

While the Czech Legion was seizing the railway in the east that summer, a different kind of war was growing in the south. In November 1917 the former commander-in-chief Mikhail Alekseyev arrived at Novocherkassk, capital of the Don Cossacks, and gathered officers and cadets into a Volunteer Army; Kornilov escaped to join him in December and took command. But the Don Cossacks were not yet willing to fight. Cossack soldiers returning from the front declared neutrality, and on 11 February 1918 the ataman Kaledin shot himself, saying that the Cossacks had abandoned their ataman. As Red Guards entered Rostov and Novocherkassk the Volunteer Army of about 4,000 men set out on 22 February for the Kuban steppe to the south: the "Ice March" that later became the founding myth of the Whites. Crossing frozen rivers in March and joining Kuban Cossack detachments, the army attacked Yekaterinodar, the Kuban capital, on 10 April, and on 13 April Kornilov was killed by a shell that struck his headquarters. Anton Denikin took command and led the remnant back to the Don.

What saved it was the Cossack rising. In March and April the requisitions and executions of the Red Guards turned the Don stanitsas around, and in April the Cossacks retook Novocherkassk. On 16 May the Cossack assembly elected Pyotr Krasnov ataman. Krasnov reached an understanding with the German army in Ukraine, which supplied the Don with more than 10,000 rifles and with guns and ammunition, paid for in grain. Denikin, who looked to the Allies, despised this "German orientation", and the quarrel between the two men marked the anti-Bolshevik south in 1918. On 23 June the Volunteer Army began its Second Kuban campaign, taking Yekaterinodar on 16 August and Novorossiysk on 26 August, which gave it a door to the Black Sea. When Alekseyev died of illness on 8 October, Denikin held both political and military authority.

Krasnov's Don Army aimed at Tsaritsyn on the Volga, the junction through which the grain of the North Caucasus and the oil of Baku reached central Russia and, for the Don, the bridge to the Ural Cossacks in the east. Three assaults, in July and August, September and October, and January 1919, all failed. The defence was remembered on the Soviet side for another reason. Stalin, who had come to Tsaritsyn in June 1918 with plenary powers over food supply, took military command into his own hands, distrusted the former imperial officers Trotsky had sent as commanders (Snesarev, Sytin) and ignored their orders. Voroshilov's 10th Army and Stalin's "Tsaritsyn group" held the city, but the clash with the high command went as far as Trotsky's demand to Lenin in October that Stalin be recalled. This conflict between military specialists and party officials would reappear on the Polish front the next year and in the army purges twenty years later.

In the winter of 1918 the southern front was redrawn by two collapses. One was that of the Red 11th Army in the North Caucasus: cut off from supply and ravaged by typhus, it disintegrated before Denikin's offensive in January 1919 and retreated across the Caspian desert to Astrakhan, tens of thousands dying on the way. The other was Germany's defeat. Deprived of his patron, Krasnov agreed on 8 January 1919 to place himself under Denikin and form the Armed Forces of South Russia, and resigned as ataman in February. The Allies arrived at Novorossiysk in November and treated Denikin as their sole partner. Thus by the spring of 1919 Denikin's army, uniting the Volunteers, the Don and the Kuban, had become the single White army of the south.

The Red Army itself then helped Denikin. On 24 January 1919 the party's Organizational Bureau ordered the elimination of the upper stratum of the Don Cossacks by "mass terror". Under this policy, known as "de-Cossackization", executions went on for weeks from stanitsa to stanitsa, and in March a Cossack rising broke out at Vyoshenskaya on the upper Don that tied down the rear of the Red southern front for two months. In June the insurgents joined Denikin's cavalry. Through that gap Denikin took Kharkiv and Yekaterinoslav in June and Tsaritsyn on 30 June, and on 3 July at Tsaritsyn issued his order to march on Moscow. That an army which had left the steppe with 4,000 men in 1918 could aim at Moscow a year later was the result not only of White strength but of the errors of Soviet power in the Cossack lands.
