## 1919: from Vilnius to Mikaszewicze

The first year's war was small, but its consequences were large. In the spring of 1919 the Polish army was still a sum of several armies: Polish officers of the old Austrian and German armies, Piłsudski's legionaries, Józef Haller's "Blue Army" of some 68,000 men organized in France and shipped in from April, and the Greater Poland army that had risen against Germany at Poznań, all being welded into one force while still wearing different uniforms and answering to different chains of command. On the Soviet side the western frontier was held by the Western Army that had followed the Germans in, always short of troops because of the southern and eastern fronts against Denikin and Kolchak.

From 19 to 21 April Piłsudski personally led cavalry in a surprise attack on Vilnius and drove out the Litbel Soviet government. In his proclamation of 22 April "to the inhabitants of the former Grand Duchy of Lithuania" he promised to let them decide their own future, but the city stayed in Polish hands. Minsk fell on 8 August, and in the autumn the front came to rest on the Berezina and the Daugava. Piłsudski stopped not because his strength was spent. That autumn Denikin, marching on Moscow, asked Poland for joint operations but refused to discuss Poland's eastern frontier except on the premise of restoring "Russia one and indivisible". Piłsudski judged that a White victory would return Poland to the borders of 1914. From October to December secret talks were held at Mikaszewicze station in Belorussia between the Polish envoy Boerner and the Soviet envoy Julian Marchlewski, and as a result the Polish army stood still during the decisive weeks in which Denikin broke at Orel. The Soviet leadership could shift some 40,000 men from the west to the south. Denikin later counted this silence among the causes of his defeat.

The truce of Mikaszewicze was not a peace. On 28 January 1920 the Soviet government offered Poland peace "on the present front line", and the Polish government put off its answer. Meanwhile on 3 January Polish troops took Daugavpils together with Latvia, and Piłsudski prepared for a decision in the spring. The Soviet side reckoned the same way. In March 1920, with Kolchak shot and Denikin embarking at Novorossiysk, the Red Army's best formations began to move west. Each country believed the other was preparing to attack, and both were right.

## Warsaw: the battles of August

On 24 July a government of national defence was formed in Warsaw with the peasant leader Wincenty Witos as prime minister and the socialist Ignacy Daszyński as his deputy. This government of left and right together appealed for volunteers, and by mid-August more than 100,000 had enlisted. The minister of military affairs, Kazimierz Sosnkowski, ran the mobilization of the rear, and the chief of staff, Tadeusz Rozwadowski, fixed the counterattack plan in operational order 8358/III of 6 August. In the north Sikorski's 5th Army would hold the Soviet 4th and 15th Armies on the Wkra; the 1st Army in front of Warsaw would hold Radzymin; and a strike group of five divisions assembled on the Wieprz in the south would drive north into the exposed left flank of the Soviet 16th Army. On 12 August Piłsudski briefly laid down the office of head of state and took personal command of the strike group.

Poland's greatest weapon was out of sight. Jan Kowalewski of the general staff's cipher bureau had been breaking the Red Army's radio ciphers since September 1919, and during the August battles the Polish command read Tukhachevsky's orders almost in real time. On 13 August the Soviet 3rd and 16th Armies attacked Radzymin, 20 kilometres east of Warsaw, taking and losing the town twice. On 14 August the priest Ignacy Skorupka, leading a volunteer battalion at Ossów, was killed, and his death soon became the central symbol of the "Miracle on the Vistula". The same day Sikorski's 5th Army counterattacked on the Wkra in the north, and on 15 August Polish cavalry raided Ciechanów, where the Soviet 4th Army's radio station stood, cutting Tukhachevsky's link to that army. For the next several days the 4th Army kept marching west without orders from headquarters.

At dawn on 16 August the strike group on the Wieprz moved north. The expected resistance was not there. The 100-kilometre gap held by the 8,000 men of the Mozyr Group opened within a day; on 17 August Polish troops took Siedlce and Mińsk Mazowiecki and on the 18th cut the railway to Białystok. Tukhachevsky, at his headquarters in Minsk, grasped the situation only on 18 August and ordered a general retreat. The 4th Army and Gaya Gai's 3rd Cavalry Corps, which had advanced furthest north of Warsaw, found their way back blocked and crossed into East Prussia between 24 and 26 August, where German troops disarmed them. When the battle ended on 25 August the Red Army had lost 66,000 men taken prisoner and more than 40,000 interned, along with more than 200 guns and 1,000 machine guns; Poland lost about 4,500 killed and 22,000 wounded.

In the south another battle followed. Budyonny's First Cavalry Army, having given up Lwów and turned north-west on 20 August, attacked Zamość from 29 to 31 August. The town was held by Polish units and the 6th Division of the Ukrainian People's Republic under Marko Bezruchko. On 31 August at Komarów Rómmel's Polish 1st Cavalry Division met the First Cavalry Army head-on, in what is called the largest cavalry battle in Europe since 1813, and Budyonny's army suffered its first decisive defeat, falling back beyond the Bug. The Poles had been reading Soviet dispositions through radio interception and code-breaking, the supply lines were stretched to breaking, and coordination with the South-Western Front had collapsed. The refusal of its political commissar, Stalin, to countersign the order transferring the Cavalry Army became the core of the later controversy over blame, and the feud between Tukhachevsky and the Stalin-Budyonny camp ran on to 1937.

French arms, ammunition, military training and attached advisers provided sustained support. The French Military Mission active from 1919 should be distinguished from the Anglo-French Interallied Mission of summer 1920. Maxime Weygand, part of the latter, advised the Polish staff and discussed the counterattack. Attributing victory to him alone would erase the planning and execution by Piłsudski, Rozwadowski and the Polish command. Conversely, the Polish "miracle" narrative erases the worldly conditions of the division in the Soviet command, the coolness of Polish workers and the broken ciphers.

## Workers' strikes and the choices of the powers: the war's international context

This war was also the first occasion on which Europe's labour movement directly blocked its own governments' foreign policy. In May 1920 dockers in the port of London refused to load the freighter Jolly George, which carried arms for Poland. On 9 August, as the Red Army approached Warsaw and the British government considered intervention, the Labour Party and the Trades Union Congress set up a "Council of Action" and declared that a war on Soviet Russia would be met with a general strike; some 350 local councils of action sprang up across the country. Lloyd George's government gave up the idea of sending troops. Germany declared neutrality on 20 July and closed its territory to transit, and in August the dockers of Danzig refused to unload munitions for Poland, so that British soldiers had to do the unloading themselves. Czechoslovakia also banned the transit of arms, and the troops offered by Horthy's Hungary were stopped by that ban. Poland fought all but isolated from the west.

The exception was France. France had kept a military mission training the Polish army since 1919, and in 1920 it sent hundreds of thousands of rifles, thousands of machine guns, hundreds of guns, aircraft and tanks. The Kościuszko Squadron, flown by American pilots, attacked Budyonny's cavalry from the air. Nor was Allied policy of one piece. At the Spa conference on 10 July Britain promised the Polish prime minister Grabski mediation on condition that Poland withdraw to the Curzon line and hand Vilnius to Lithuania, and on 11 July the Curzon note went to Moscow; the Soviet government, sure of victory, refused it. France saw Poland as a barrier between Germany and the Soviets; Britain wanted trade with Russia reopened. The victory of August freed Poland from the conditions of either.

On the Soviet side the war was the Comintern's first field test. The delegates to the Second Congress of the Comintern, meeting in Moscow from 19 July to 7 August, followed the movement of the front on a map hung in the hall. The "Twenty-One Conditions" the congress adopted and its theses on the national and colonial question rested on the premise that Europe's revolution was imminent. The fact that Polish workers did not greet the Red Army as liberators shook that premise. Karl Radek and the Polish communists had warned of the force of Polish nationalism before the advance, and Lenin later admitted that the war had been "a reconnaissance by bayonet". The idea of hastening Europe's revolution by military means ended before Warsaw, and trade agreements, the Rapallo treaty and the New Economic Policy took its place.
